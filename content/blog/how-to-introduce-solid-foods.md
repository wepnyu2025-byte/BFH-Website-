Deciding when and how to start complementary feeding can feel overwhelming for many new mothers and fathers. You might wonder whether your infant is truly ready, which local foods are safest to start with, or how to avoid digestive distress. 

With simple preparation, patience, and clear guidance aligned with World Health Organization standards, introducing solid foods becomes one of the most rewarding milestones in your parenting journey. ### Key Takeaways
- **Wait until around 6 months:** Exclusive breastfeeding or formula feeding supplies all the fluids and nutrients an infant requires for the first six months of life.
- **Look for developmental readiness:** Your baby must be able to hold their head steady without wobbling, sit upright with minimal support, and show active interest in your food.
- **Start with single-ingredient purees:** Introduce one single ingredient at a time and wait two to three days before adding another to easily track potential food intolerances.
- **Never force-feed:** Responsive feeding means following your child's hunger and fullness cues rather than finishing an arbitrary quantity.
- **Continue breast milk or formula:** Milk feeds remain the primary source of calories and immunity throughout the first year.

## When Is Your Baby Ready for Solid Foods?

The World Health Organization (WHO) and pediatric health guidelines universally recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life. Around six months, an infant's digestive system matures enough to digest starches and proteins, their tongue-thrust reflex naturally diminishes, and their iron stores begin to need dietary replenishment.

Before placing the first spoonful near your baby's lips, look for these three clear physical milestones:

1. **Strong head and neck control:** Your baby can sit upright in a high chair or on your lap and hold their head steady without fatigue.
2. **Loss of the extrusion reflex:** When food is placed on their tongue, they no longer push it straight out with their tongue reflex.
3. **Intentional hand-to-mouth coordination:** The baby leans forward toward food, opens their mouth eagerly when a spoon approaches, and grasps objects with coordination.

Age alone is not the only indicator. If your infant is 5 months old but cannot support their neck steadily, their airway is not yet protected for swallowing semi-solids. Always wait until both age and developmental readiness align.

## Safe and Nutritious First Foods to Offer

Your baby's first meals should be soft, completely smooth, and prepared without added salt, refined sugar, honey, or artificial seasoning cubes. An infant's developing kidneys cannot handle excess sodium, and honey carries a serious risk of infant botulism under 12 months.

### 1. Iron-Rich Purees
At six months, the natural iron stores infants accumulate before birth begin to dip. Ideal first options include:
- Pureed cooked liver or well-steamed tender meat pureed to a silk texture.
- Cooked, mashed yellow lentils or well-strained beans.
- Iron-fortified single-grain baby porridges made with breast milk.

### 2. Gentle Local Vegetables and Tubers
Soft root vegetables and mild tubers are easily digested and gentle on tender stomachs:
- Steamed sweet potatoes or carrots pureed with a splash of warm breast milk or boiled clean water.
- Steamed pumpkin or butternut squash.
- Mild steamed plantain puree.

### 3. Mashed Local Fruits
Naturally sweet fruits provide potassium, vitamin C, and fiber:
- Ripe avocado mashed smooth with a fork (healthy plant fats help rapid brain development).
- Ripe sweet banana mashed thoroughly.
- Steamed apple or ripe papaya puree.

## How to Conduct the 3-Day Waiting Rule

When introducing any new food item—especially potential allergens like eggs, fish, groundnut paste, or dairy—introduce only **one new food item every 2 to 3 days**.

Keep a simple notebook or phone note. Note down what time you offered the food, how much was consumed, and your baby's skin and bowel patterns over the next 48 hours. By isolating each new ingredient, you can immediately identify what caused a reaction if your child develops hives, sudden vomiting, facial swelling, or severe diarrhea.

## The Responsive Feeding Approach

Feeding your infant is a conversation, not a task to rush through. Responsive feeding promotes a lifelong positive relationship with healthy nutrition.

- Sit directly in front of your baby so they can see your warm facial expressions.
- Hold a small spoonful of puree about 20 to 30 cm from their mouth. Wait for them to lean forward and open their mouth before offering the food.
- Never force the spoon into a closed mouth.
- Watch for fullness signals: turning the head away, pushing the spoon with hands, arching the back, or clamping the lips shut.
- Expect mess: touching food with their fingers is an essential sensory milestone that teaches babies texture and hand-eye control.

## Foods to Avoid During the First Year

To keep your child safe from choking and illness, strictly avoid:
- **Honey:** Never give honey to a child under 12 months due to the risk of *Clostridium botulinum* spores.
- **Added salt and stock cubes:** Overburdens fragile kidneys.
- **Added sugar and sweetened juices:** Promotes early dental decay and displaces nutritious milk feeds.
- **Whole nuts, whole grapes, and raw hard carrot rounds:** Major choking hazards. Always puree, mash, or finely grind. For emergency awareness and airway safety, review our [Childhood Emergency Guide](/products/childhood-emergency-guide).
- **Unpasteurized dairy products:** Carries risks of harmful foodborne bacteria.

## When to See a Doctor

While minor changes in stool color and texture are normal when solids begin, certain warning signs require immediate medical attention.

Contact your healthcare provider or visit the nearest clinic promptly if you notice:
- **Hives, swelling of the lips, eyes, or tongue, or wheezing/coughing:** Call emergency services immediately as this indicates a severe allergic reaction.
- **Repeated forceful vomiting within two hours of a feed.**
- **Blood or persistent mucus in stools.**
- **Signs of dehydration:** No wet diapers for over 6 hours, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or unusual lethargy.
- **Persistent refusal of all fluids and feeds for more than 24 hours.**
